### Abstract

Abstract Testing software product lines represents a challenging task mainly because there are many derivable products. To facilitate this issue, multiple solutions were developed to reduce the number of products that are tested while maintaining a good percentage of coverage. However, the order of testing products has received little consideration. The purpose of this research is twofold: first, to replicate the results of a previous study (which uses two specific metrics for prioritization, namely, Variability Coverage &amp; Cyclomatic Complexity - VC&amp;CC, and Coefficient of Connectivity-Density - CoC), and second, to investigate two new metrics to be used as prioritization criteria (Ratio of Variability - RoV, and Flexibility of Configuration - FoC). The APFD (Average Percentage of Faults Detected) metric is used to evaluate the results obtained. In the investigation, a set of 9 feature models with various numbers of features, grouped in three intervals, was used. The results show that the original findings are confirmed for all feature models used. Regarding the new criteria used, FoC and RoV outperformed the CoC metric in 6 out of 9 cases, and also obtained the best results in 3 out of 9 cases. In the other 6 out of 9 cases the VC&amp;CC criterion obtained the best results.

Introduction
Software Product Line (SPL) testing is challenging due to the potentially huge number of derivable products. To facilitate this problem, numerous contributions (Sánchez et al. 2014;Lopez-Herrejon et al. 2013) have been proposed to reduce the number of products to be tested while still having good coverage. However, not much attention has been paid to the order in which the products are tested.
Quality of software can be assured by going through the software testing (Offutt 1998) process of the Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC). Smarter software testing can help deliver a product, which is more reliable and defect-free, meeting the business requirements and stakeholders' expectations. This is the reason why it takes more time and resources and therefore, making this process very expensive in terms of resources, time and costs. Due to the limited time left when the product reaches the Testing stage, it is important to prioritize the test cases.
Test Case Prioritization (TCP) (Elbaum et al. 2014) involves ranking test cases within a test suite based on a variety of criteria, such as code coverage, functionality, or the assessment of risk and critical features. This method allows for executing the most important test cases initially, which can lead to earlier detection of errors and timely feedback for testers.
During the last decade, a great interest for test case prioritization in software product lines has been acknowledged (Sánchez et al. 2014;Lopez-Herrejon et al. 2013). Such an approach starts with creating a dataset of product lines that simulates real ones. Then, a set of test cases is generated and is subject to the process of prioritization. It is assumed that the test cases with the highest priority find an important part of the faults in the testing phase.
The aim of this work is twofold: (1) to replicate the experiments in the previous approach (Sánchez et al. 2014), and (2) to investigate the use of new metrics as prioritization criteria, namely, Ratio Of Variability metric and Flexibility of Configuration metric. These metrics were selected, after investigation of all metrics in Bagheri and Gasevic (2011), since they may have impact also on the test cases designed when there are optional features available for designers while configuring the feature model. Our goal is to order a set of test cases based on the metrics implemented, which resembles the intuition of a tester.
The motivation for replication is based on three strong, research-grounded ways corresponding to different perspectives: theoretical, methodological, and contextual relevance. Thus, the original study provided a valuable foundation by demonstrating that prioritization strategies leveraging feature model information can enhance fault detection effectiveness. However, the robustness and generalizability of those findings remain to be examined under alternative formulations and contexts. From a theoretical standpoint, the replication is motivated by the need to validate whether the conceptual relationship between feature coverage and prioritization performance persists when new metrics are employed. From a methodological perspective, the experimental procedures of the original work, although sound, were limited to a single SPL configuration and specific metric definitions. Replicating and extending these procedures using two new metrics allows for a systematic assessment of methodological reproducibility and the reliability of the underlying evaluation pipeline.

Finally, from a contextual viewpoint, the inclusion of three sets of SPLs with distinct feature model characteristics enables an empirical examination of the external validity of the original conclusions. By combining these perspectives, this replication aims to provide a deeper and more generalizable understanding of how variability information, metric design, and SPL structure interact to influence the effectiveness of test case prioritization.

Background on test case prioritization in software product lines
This section is split into three parts. First, the background of test case prioritization and its definition are presented. Then, details regarding software product lines and the way of representing them are offered, concluding with information about testing software product lines.
Test case prioritization
TCP is formally defined (see Definition 1 below) as the process of ordering the execution of test cases so that the most important tests are executed earlier.
Definition 1 Definition 1: Test Case Prioritization (Graves et al. 2001): a test suite, T, the set of permutations of T, PT; a function from PT to real numbers, f. The goal is to find T ∈ PT such that:
(∀T ′′ )(T ′′ ∈ P T )(T ′′ ̸ = T ′ )[f (T ′ ) ≥ f (T ′′ )],(1)
where the function f assigns a real value to a permutation of T according to the test adequacy of the particular permutation.
Several ordering criteria can be suggested, depending on the intended objective of the testers. The test cases may be arranged, for instance, according to the amount of errors found in earlier runs of the test suite or according to how likely it is that the components being tested are error-prone. This would help in increasing the rate of fault detection.
Software product lines
The central element in the case of software product lines is feature model, which is described in what follows. Nesic et al. (2019) provide more details about feature models.
Feature models are frequently used to represent SPLs. The features, which specify the capabilities and behavior of a software system, serve as a means of product differentiation in SPL. Feature models provide a structure similar to a tree by recording all possible SPL products' attributes and connections between them.
Figure 1 shows an instance of a feature model that depicts an online commerce SPL. The nodes of the tree represent the features, which are depicted as labeled boxes, and the relations between them are represented by the edges.
Every feature (apart from the root) has a single parent feature and may have multiple child features. We can observe that in a valid product configuration, a child feature can appear only if the parent feature is chosen. Also, in a valid configuration, the root feature will always appear. Inside a feature model we can have four types of relationships: Beside parental relations between features, cross-tree constraints are allowed. There are two types: Requires (feature1 requires feature2 = choosing feature1 in a product involves also choosing feature2), Excludes (feature1 excludes feature2 = feature1 and feature2 cannot be part of the same product). In our Fig. 1, we have Credit Card feature Requires High feature and High feature Excludes Public report feature.
Testing SPL
Testing SPL is a more challenging than testing single systems. Ideal would be to test each product individually, but unfortunately is too expensive in practice. In fact, the number of products derived from a software product line increases exponentially when the number of features grows, which can lead to thousand or even millions of different products. In this context, the need of prioritizing test cases appeared.

Related work
In this section, we briefly survey existing test case prioritization approaches with focus on software product lines and related work in this area.
A systematic literature review (SLR) (Kitchenham 2004) was conducted with the aim to indicate the state-of-the-art and to discover the on-going scientific research on TCP-related algorithms that apply on software product lines. The search was conducted on two databases and one indexing system. We defined several appropriate keywords (test case prioritization, combinatorial testing, test selection approaches, fault detection, code coverage, prioritization criterion, feature models, system product lines), based on the aspects we want to focus our research on. The search results were merged together, having duplicates and entries which were not research papers removed. Also, we chose papers published starting with the year 2000. Twenty-eight studies were found to be relevant for our work. In what follows, we provide detailed descriptions for some of them, a detailed analysis being available in the thesis (Tiutin 2024). Sanchez et al. (2014) present five prioritization criteria whose goal is to maximize the SPL suite's early error detection rate. Three of them are dependent on how complex they are, meaning that the complex products are tested first (it is assumed that they are more prone to errors). The degree to which product features can be reused serves as the basis for the fourth criterion. Thus, high-risk faults that impact a significant percentage of products are found early. Lastly, the fifth criterion is based on the dissimilarity between products (how different are two products). Its assumption is that the rate of error detection increases with the number of distinct products. To evaluate how fast the faults found in the testing phase are, the APFD metric is used. The results demonstrate that the rates of early error detection offered by various prioritization criteria vary significantly. At the same time, the results point out that the criteria used can help in accelerating the detection of fault of both pairwise or random software product line test suites.
Alba et al. ( 2013) tackle a zero-one mathematical linear program to solve the multi-objective problem (maximize coverage and minimize the test suite size) plus an algorithm which uses SAT solvers to compute for a feature model the true Pareto front. The Parento front represents the best solution to achieve both goals. Using 118 feature models, they evaluated the approach and managed to obtain the Pareto front. After evaluating the approach, they revealed a relation between the quantity of products in the feature model and runtime.
A goal-oriented approach is presented by Biletskiy et al. (2011), an approach that selects the most important features of the SPL. This method makes it possible to determine which features are more crucial and require more thorough testing. Therefore, for reducing the test cases' number, they carried out two steps: first, they determined which features, in the opinion of domain stakeholders, are more significant; second, based on the resources at their disposal, they chose the top test cases. The results demonstrate how the approach may efficiently prioritize the test cases that remain while also drastically reducing the test space.
A relevant approach to our work is provided by Sanchez et al. (2014). They present five prioritization criteria whose goal is to maximize the SPL suite's early error detection rate (faults are detected as fast as possible): Dissimilarity, Commonality, Coefficient of Connectivity-Density, Variability Coverage & Cyclomatic Complexity, and Cross-Tree-Constrains Ratio (Schmid et al. 2017). To evaluate how fast the faults are detected, the metric APFD is used. The results demonstrate that the rates of early fault detection offered by various priority criteria vary significantly.
Henard et al. ( 2014) uses similarity-based prioritization that complements the feature-metric ordering emphasis by Sanchez et al. ( 2014) by addressing variability coverage via t-wise coverage and similarity heuristics, a core to cohesion/ connectivity via feature interaction coverage. Hierons et al. (2020) extends the multi-objective prioritization idea (feature/interaction coverage) that Sanchez et al. ( 2014) motivated for ordering SPL tests by using pairwise interaction coverage (variability coverage) as core objective, enabling combining structural and variability objectives.

A Software Product Line (SPL) is a set of software products that are built from a variability model. Real-world SPLs typically involve a vast number of valid products, making it impossible to individually test each of them. This arises the need for automated test suite generation, which was previously modeled as either a single-objective or a multi-objective optimization problem considering only objective functions. This article provides a completely different mathematical model by exploiting the benefits of Quality-Diversity (QD) optimization that is composed of not only an objective function (e.g., t-wise coverage or test suite diversity) but also a user-defined behavior space (e.g., the space with test suite size as its dimension). We argue that the new model is more suitable and generic than the two alternatives because it provides at a time a large set of diverse (measured in the behavior space) and high-performing solutions that can ease the decision-making process. We apply MAP-Elites, one of the most popular QD algorithms, to solve the model. The results of the evaluation, on both realistic and artificial SPLs, are promising, with MAP-Elites significantly and substantially outperforming both single-and multi-objective approaches, and also several state-of-the-art SPL testing tools. In summary, this article provides a new and promising perspective on the test suite generation for SPLs.
Our approach builds on the implementation of Sanchez et al. ( 2014), by introducing two new prioritization criteria: Ratio of Variability (RoV) and Flexibility of Configuration (FoV), which will be detailed in the following sections. In comparision to other studies, our approach share the same overarching goal, that of improvements of the effectiveness of test case prioritization in SPL testing by defining measurable criteria that enable early fault detection. However, the nature and granularity of the prioritization criteria differ substantially. Several approaches as those mentioned above, are centered on feature similarity and diversity-based criteria, evaluating approaches such as pairwise dissimilarity, random ordering, and cost-aware selection, primarily focusing on maximizing coverage of distinct feature combinations. In contrast, our approach, by using the RoV and FoV metrics, introduces structural and evolutionary dimensions of variability as prioritization factors.
Replication in software engineering
Replication is a core component of experimental methods, involving the repetition of experiments to verify earlier outcomes. It is crucial to validate these previous findings. Shepperd et al. (2018) identified multiple criteria that need to be satisfied for experiments to qualify as replications. These aspects are: the authors need to specify the original experiments that are to be replicated; replication studies should incorporate experiments that enable the extension of external validity; both experiments need to share the same research questions (common structures and interventions), and the analysis should compare the original results with the new ones, determining whether they are consistent or inconsistent with the original experiment.
The methodology for replicating software engineering experiments has been explored by Carver (2010) and Carver et al. (2014), which suggest four key components that could be incorporated into the replication report: (1) details on the initial research, offering enough background to comprehend the process of replication; (2) acquaint readers with information about replication to aid their comprehension of the distinct critical aspects of the replication process; (3) highlight similarities and distinctions between the initial findings and those derived from the replication, and (4), the conclusions gathered from multiple studies provide readers with significant insights that might not be apparent from just one study.
Four distinct aspects of replication identified by Gomez et al. ( 2014): operationalization, population, protocol, and experimenters. The importance of thorough documentation in replication designs is emphasized Fagerholm et al. (2019) as follows:
• The Operationalization dimension refers to the process of converting a construct into its tangible form. Cause constructs are turned into treatments (which indicate the similarity in replication compared to baseline experiments), and effect constructs are translated into metrics and methods of measurement. • The Population dimension pertains to the subjects of the experiment. Replications explore the limits of the properties of these experimental subjects to determine the validity of the results. • The Protocol dimension pertains to the experimental components that can vary over time in replication, including experimental design, subjects, guidelines, measurement tools, and data analysis methods. • The dimension concerning the experimenters pertains to the individuals participating in the study. The study by Gomez et al. (2014) indicates that in the case of replication, the outcomes should remain consistent regardless of who conducted the experiment or the roles the participants assumed.
The dimensions mentioned previously are integrated into our replication strategy by embedding the previously described dimensions, details being provided in Section 5.1. In summary, replication in software engineering is crucial, serving both to validate the results and to uncover further insights about the replicated method.

Software product line testing process
The testing process of SPL as provided by Sanchez et al. ( 2014) is represented in Fig. 3, which is composed by several steps. As outlined in Section 2, each software product line is represented as a feature model, having a tree structure. A structure like this is responsible for keeping track of the details about the products, including their features and relationships between them. Thus, the first step in the testing process is creating the feature model associated with the SPL. From it, we can derive the list of possible products.
As represented in Fig. 3, in the second step the test cases are selected. Testing a SPL constitute a more complex and challenging task than testing a single system. An ideal scenario would be to test each product separately, but the cost of the procedure would increase drastically. However, if the number of features would grow, the number of products would increase exponentially, reaching numbers of the order of thousands or even millions. Thus, it became necessary to diminish the testing space. The choice of test cases plans to reduce the set of feature combinations to an acceptable yet ideal set of products for obtaining an important coverage of feature interactions. Our approach will generate random the test suite, simulating an engineer's manual test selection who chooses which products to test based on different factors like: marketing strategy, release plan, cost and so on.
After the second step, we will obtain the SPL test suite, which will be further subject to the process of TCP. TCP approaches reorder the test cases belonging to a test suite in order to increase their effectiveness for reaching a performance target. There can be multiple targets of prioritization like: increasing the rate of detecting faults, reducing the cost of testing. Thus, starting from a prioritization target, many prioritization criteria can be applied on the suite for reaching the target proposed. In the following, we will consider four prioritization criteria used in our research.
Metrics for feature models
According to Schmid et al. (2017), there are multiple metrics associated with a feature model. The metrics investigated are described in what follows.

Sanchez et al. ( 2014) used two metrics for feature models that were incorporated into the test case prioritization approach. The first one is Coefficient of Connectivity-Density metric (CoC). Bagheri et al. (2011) define it as the ratio of the number of edges over the total number of features. In graph theory, the coefficient of connectivity represents how well the graph components are connected.
For the feature model (fm) from Fig. 1, edges(fm) = 11 and available(fm) = 10, thus CoC(fm) = 11/10 = 1.1.

The second metric is Variability Coverage & Cyclomatic Complexity proposed by Ensan et al. (2012) and used in the original replicated paper by Sanchez et al. (2014). Under this criterion, products with greater function values are considered more efficient at detecting faults and are prioritized for testing. The cyclomatic complexity represents the overall count of cycles of a feature model. Since a feature model is a tree, cycles can only be created by cross-tree constraints. We have investigated the metrics by Bagheri et al. (2011) and decided to select two new metrics that may have impact also on the test cases designed when there are optional features available for designers while configuring the feature model.

Therefore, we have selected metrics that considers variability and flexibility of configurations.
The first proposed metric is Ratio of Variability. Bagheri et al. (2011) define it as the feature model's average branching factor of parent features. Put differently, the average number of children of a parent node. We have used the definition constructed in El-Sharkawy et al. ( 2017), the same metric being used by Berger et al. (2014), by Mefteh et al. (2015), and by Maâzoun et al. (2016).
RoV (fm) = |children(fm)| |parents(fm)| (4) children(fm) = {f c | f c ∈ f m & f c is not the root f eature} parents(fm) = {f p | f p ∈ f m & f p is not a leaf f eature}
For the feature model (fm) in Fig. 1, parents(fm) = 4 and children(fm) = 9, so RoV(fm) = 9/4 = 2.25.
The RoV metric indicates the average branching factor of parent features within the feature model. In simpler terms, it refers to the average count of child nodes in the feature model's tree structure.
The second metric used is Flexibility of Configuration. Again, Bagheri et al. ( 2011) define it as the ratio of the number of optional features over the number of available features as provided in (5). The reasoning for this is that designers have more options open to them, when presented with more optional features within the feature model. Thus, it leads to a configuration process with higher complexity.
F oC(fm) = |optional(fm)| |available(fm)|
(5)
In the case of the feature model (fm) from Fig. 1, optional(fm) = 2 and available(fm) = 10, thus FoC(fm) = 2/10 = 0.2. The RoC metric is calculated by taking the ratio of optional features to the total features present in the feature model. This metric highlights that a higher number of optional features within a feature model provides designers with greater flexibility and choices during the configuration process.
While existing metrics, Coefficient of Connectivity Density and Variability Coverage & Cyclomatic Complexity, capture structural or control-flow aspects of SPL test artifacts, they offer limited insight into how variability distribution and configurational freedom influence test case effectiveness. To address this gap, we use two complementary metrics, Ratio of Variability (RoV) and Flexibility of Configuration (FoC), designed to quantify variability from a product-line perspective. The RoV metric characterizes the relative proportion of variable features within a configuration space, reflecting how variability intensity may affect prioritization outcomes. The FoC metric measures the degree of configurational flexibility, i.e., the number of feasible configurations influenced by individual features, indicating how feature dependencies shape testing opportunities.
Dataset

We have used the same faults and generated files using the fault generator for feature models as provided in Sánchez et al. (2014). The fault generator is designed to simulate faults in n-tuples of features, where n ∈ [1, 4] and represents the number of features included in the n-tuple. These faults replicate interaction faults that necessitate a specific set of features to be revealed. Research indicates that faults resulting from the interaction of between 2 and 4 features occur frequently in practice Kuhn et al. (2004). In each model, the number of faults introduced is equivalent to m=10, where m represents the number of model features. This information, the faults, is specified in the last column of Table 1. Faults were distributed evenly across types, comprising 25% in single features, 25% in pairs of features, 25% in triples of features, and 25% in quadruples of features. Consequently, the generator takes a feature model as input and outputs a randomized list of faulty feature sets.

Successful replication enhances the credibility of the findings obtained from the experiments. Performing a replication study as provided by Tore et al. Results overview and discussions
We can observe that the metric with the best results is VC&CC. In 6 out of 9 cases (66.66 %) it obtained the highest value. Also, for each feature model regardless of their suite size, we obtained at least one value greater than 94.

The CoC metric obtained values of at least 90 in 7 out of 9 cases (77.77 %), but only once obtained the best result. So out of those four prioritization criteria (VC&CC, CoC, FoC, RoV), it had the lowest performance.
The fifth prioritization criteria used is Random. We can observed that it did not perform very well, obtaining only four times values over 90. At the same time, in 7 out of 9 cases obtained the worst result.
The findings of the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank test outline that there are statistical significant differences between Random approach and all the other approaches (VC&CC, CoC, FoC, RoV). Results implications for practitioners and researchers
The empirical results show that the newly introduced Flexibility of Configuration (FoC) and Ratio of Variability (RoV) metrics achieved highly competitive performance in test case prioritization for software product lines (SPLs).
The findings suggest that the FoC and RoV metrics provide a reliable and cost-effective alternative to existing prioritization strategies, capturing aspects of configuration diversity and adaptability that traditional cost-based metrics fail to represent.
For practitioners, this implies that incorporating these metrics into regression or continuous integration pipelines can lead to higher fault detection rates at earlier testing stages without requiring significant additional analysis effort. Because both metrics rely on structural properties of feature models and configuration variability rather than runtime data, they can be readily automated in SPL test management tools and integrated into model-driven testing frameworks to guide test execution sequencing more effectively.
From a research perspective, the results indicate that variability-and flexibilityaware metrics offer a promising new dimension in test case prioritization research. While the VC&CC criterion still achieved the overall best performance, the competitive results of FoC and RoV highlight the potential of metrics that combine structural variability analysis with configuration elasticity measures. This opens several research directions, including hybrid prioritization strategies that integrate variability coverage, configuration flexibility, and execution history into multi-objective formulations.
Threats to validity
The possible risks to the efficacy of our research are covered in this section.
Internal validity refers to factors that could have influenced the obtained results.
To prevent any bias in the implementation and ensure the reproducibility of our work, we used an available tool (SPLAR) for creating the dataset. Since there are not any actual software product lines with accessible test cases, we evaluated our research by simulating faults in several SPL of different sizes which are either public or random generated. A potential internal validity threat concerns the possible mis-specification or incomplete formalization of the FoC and RoV metrics, as any inaccuracies in how variability or configuration flexibility were quantified could have influenced the observed prioritization performance and led to biased comparisons with existing criteria. However, we have used the same definitions as in previous studies.
Construct validity is about verifying if the proposed construct is real and reflects its target construct. Regarding intentional validity (the constructs adequately represent what we intend to study), we studied the prioritization of test cases in software product lines using feature models reported online. Regarding the validity of the representation (how well do the constructs or abstractions translate into observable measures), we used feature models with different number of features (up to 100 features, up to 300 features and up to 500 features). A possible construct validity threat arises from the assumption that the FoC and RoV metrics accurately serve as proxies for fault detection effectiveness, since their computed variability and flexibility values may not perfectly reflect the actual fault-revealing potential of prioritized test cases. To mitigate this risk, future studies could validate these metrics empirically by correlating their prioritization outcomes with real or injected fault data (e.g., through mutation analysis or seeded fault experiments) to assess the extent to which they truly capture fault-detection capability.
External validity is related to the generalization of the obtained results. For evaluation we took into consideration only one project (Sánchez et al. 2014). In order to reduce threats to external validity we chose a project whose dataset is composed of features models of different sizes (up to 100 features, up to 300 features and up to 500 features). We plan to continue the experimental evaluation by creating a larger dataset and to analyze other projects as well. Also, we would like to use new metrics which could impact the priority of the test cases which care executed. A potential external validity threat stems from the use of a limited dataset and synthetic feature models, which may not fully represent the complexity, variability, and evolution patterns of real-world software product lines, thereby limiting the generalizability of the results to industrial contexts.

The paper covers multiple aspects. It contains a theoretic chapter which presents the background of our topic, performed using the systematic literature review methodology. This paper proposes a new method to prioritize test cases in SPL. We implemented four prioritization criteria, two of them being used in other papers (Coefficient of Connectivity-Density and Variability Coverage & Cyclomatic Complexity), while two of them are newly used (Ratio Of Variability and Flexibility of Configuration).
For validating the approach, the dataset used is composed of nine feature models which have different sizes: three of them have 50-100 features, three of them have 100-300 features, and three of them have 300-500 features. Each feature model corresponds to a software product line. Following, we generated random the test suite, which is further subject to the process of prioritizing test case. To evaluate the approach we used the APFD metric.
The metric with the best results is Variability Coverage & Cyclomatic Complexity, which obtained the highest value in 6 out of 9 cases. The metrics Flexibility of Configuration and Ratio Of Variability obtained very close results and outperformed the Coefficient of Connectivity-Density metric in 6 out of 9 cases. At the same time, they obtained the best results in 3 out of 9 cases. The Coefficient of Connectivity-Density metric only once obtained the best result. So out of those four prioritization criteria, it had the lowest performance.
As future work, creating a larger dataset containing real feature models of different sizes with real test cases to further analyze the results would be important/useful. Likewise, investigating new metrics for prioritizing test cases would also advance the TCP for SPL research. Additionally, new experiments that consider hybrid prioritization strategies would also be designed and operationalized, for example considering various weights for variability coverage, configuration flexibility, and execution history.
